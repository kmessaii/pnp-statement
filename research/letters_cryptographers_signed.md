## 2. Five most relevant, in more detail

Chosen for relevance to a letter that asks researchers to restrain a line of their own or others' technical work, backed by technical authority. Items 2 and 4 below are the closest to a document a cryptographer signs on technical merit.

**A. Mirror life, Science 2024 (the closest "restrain our own work" precedent).** Opens with a claim of unprecedented risk: mirror bacteria could evade natural immune defences and spread through ecosystems (Axios, Bulletin). It does not call for stopping the whole field: research on mirror molecules can continue. It names a concrete line (creating the organism), gives a timeline (at least a decade away), and asks funders for a public pledge, which is a cheap, checkable action. It reads as a warning from the people with the most knowledge of the field, with Nobel laureates among the 38. Dissent existed (Ellington: mirror bacteria would be at a competitive disadvantage; Scientific American). I did not find an organiser retrospective in this session; the Bulletin 2026 follow-up exists and was not read. Sources: https://www.axios.com/2024/12/17/mirror-life-ban-scientists ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/creating-mirror-life-could-be-disastrous-scientists-warn/

**B. Keys Under Doormats (2015).** Frames itself as a sequel: "Twenty years ago, law enforcement organizations lobbied to require data and communication services" (to provide access), and says the damage would be greater today (MIT page, exact excerpts). The argument is technical: exceptional access would force developers to reverse forward secrecy practices. It ends with unresolved questions on governance and human rights, not a demand. It did not claim law enforcement has no legitimate need (summary on the MIT page). Organisers' retrospective: NOT FOUND. Source: https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/97690

**C. 1997 key recovery report.** Takes a deliberately narrow stance. The page says such systems are "inherently less secure, more costly, and more difficult to use" than systems without recovery, and "We urge public debate to carefully weigh the costs and benefits of government-access key recovery". It explicitly leaves the cost-benefit judgment to the public and policymakers and takes no side on the policy. Evidence: engineering analysis of scale and complexity. Source: https://www.schneier.com/academic/archives/1997/04/the_risks_of_key_rec.html

**D. EU scientists' letter on child sexual abuse scanning (2023 to 2025).** Opens by addressing the legislature with a recommendation that the proposal "be not taken forward" (netzpolitik paraphrase, not verified as an exact quote), then gives technical reasons: perceptual hashing can be evaded and misfire, AI classifiers produce false positives, client-side scanning weakens end-to-end encryption and "acts like spyware" (netzpolitik quoting). It offers an alternative (DSA complaint tools, education, hotlines, takedown) and welcomes the child-protection goal. It is re-issued as the text changes: the Sep 2025 version welcomed faster reporting and removal; the Nov 2025 version "explicitly welcome[d]" removal of mandatory detection and said "The potential harm and the potential for abuse are enormous – the benefit unproven" (heise, quoting). Organiser retrospective: NOT FOUND. Sources: https://netzpolitik.org/2023/wissenschaftler-warnen-chatkontrolle-ist-der-falsche-weg/ ; https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2025/09/09/csam-eu-child-abuse-regulation/ ; https://heise.de/-11085476

**E. IACR Copenhagen Resolution (2014).** The shortest and the only one adopted by a professional body. The first sentence "repudiates mass surveillance and the undermining of cryptographic solutions and standards" (IACR page, as paraphrased by the tool; treat as near-exact), then states that population-wide surveillance threatens democracy and human dignity and calls for faster research. It is a values statement plus a research agenda, with no named target. It came about because one member argued at the business meeting that silence was a failure (minutes). Sources: https://iacr.org/misc/statement-May2014.html ; https://www.iacr.org/docs/minutes/ec2014mem.pdf

(Bugs in Our Pockets and the 2020 contact-tracing statement are in the table; they follow the same pattern of a named bad design plus a named alternative or principles.)

## 3. Patterns (MY READING)

1. **The asks target other people's actions, almost always a government or company proposal.** Key escrow, exceptional access, client-side scanning, scanning laws, centralised tracing, mass surveillance. In the cryptography cases I found, none asks signatories to restrain their own research. The only restrain-our-own-work document in my set is the mirror-life piece, from biology. The one place cryptographers restrain themselves is the IACR resolution's commitment to speed up privacy research, which is the opposite direction.
2. **The authority claimed is technical impossibility or risk, not morality.** Documents say a thing cannot be done safely, will fail, or can be abused. Moral language appears (IACR: "democracy and human dignity") only in the short resolution, and even there as a conclusion, not a method.
3. **They are narrow and name a specific design, bill or protocol.** Exceptions are the IACR resolution and the AI statements, which are broad; the AI statements drew the widest criticism (section 4).
4. **Most offer an alternative or a way out:** DSA reporting and education (EU letter), decentralised protocols (contact tracing), funder pledges (mirror life), public debate (1997). Pure "no" letters are rarer.
5. **Length and tone:** technical reports are long (46 pages, 15k words); the signed petitions are short to medium; the shortest (IACR, AI statement) are one paragraph or sentence. Tone is sober and engineering-like; they concede the legitimate goal (child protection, public health, law enforcement need) before objecting.
6. **Who hosts:** author-led academic documents (MIT CSAIL, arXiv, a university news page), professional bodies (IACR), dedicated single-issue sites (csa-scientist-open-letter.org), civil-society coalitions (CDT) and think tanks or institutes for AI (FLI, CAIS). Letters that quote the same recurring set of senior names in the 1997, 2015, 2021 and 2023 documents suggest a small core of authors and a wider signing community.
7. **Persistence:** the EU letter was reissued four times as the legislative text moved, and the numbers grew (300 to about 500). A living letter with versions seems well tolerated.
8. **Effects:** policy outcomes followed in some cases (Apple abandoned its plan; the EU dropped mandatory detection), but I could not establish causation in any case. The FLI pause had no effect.

## 4. Letters not rallied behind, or criticised

- **FLI "Pause" letter (2023).** Sharp public criticism, including from researchers it cited, and from AI ethics scholars who said it neglected present harms (IEEE Spectrum https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-pause-letter-stokes-fear ; TechCrunch https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/31/ethicists-fire-back-at-ai-pause-letter-they-say-ignores-the-actual-harms). Criticism also about funding ties (Computing.co.uk, via search result). I did not find data on how many cryptographers or theoretical computer scientists signed or declined.
- **CAIS extinction statement (2023).** Criticised as hype by some (Fast Company headline in search results); Meta and its AI chief did not sign (per the search summary of Wikipedia). No evidence found about cryptographers declining.
- **Contact-tracing letter (2020).** The Register reports the letter endorsed four named decentralised projects while the French letter criticised both DP-3T and ROBERT; a later paper by Vaudenay argued the letter was used as an academic endorsement by decentralised advocates (via the search summary of https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/277809/files/ppct.pdf). Shows that naming specific designs invites a split in the signing community.
- **Post-quantum standardisation.** I found no signed open letter. There is a dispute, mainly in public mailing lists and one author's posts, about hybrid versus non-hybrid key exchange; a report says six people objected in an IETF TLS discussion while 23 others acknowledged the concerns (LWN, https://lwn.net/Articles/1048978/, via search summary). That is argument, not a petition.
- **Dual EC DRBG.** I found no group letter. The public record in my results is individual commentary and NIST's own withdrawal in April 2014 (https://www.route-fifty.com/cybersecurity/2014/04/nist-drops-crypto-algorithm/297179/).
- I found NO instance of a named cryptography or theoretical computer science body publicly declining an open letter.

## 6. Addendum, 2026-11-23: primary texts and two further searches

Method: I downloaded the two EU letter PDFs from csa-scientist-open-letter.org myself and extracted their text locally (the fetch tool could not read them). Quotations from those are exact, taken from the extraction (line breaks in the PDF were collapsed and some words in the 2023 file run together; I have restored spaces only). The Apple letter text I could only reach through a news page, and the 2020 statement not at all.

### 6.1 EU scientists' letter, 4 July 2023 (primary text read)
Source: https://csa-scientist-open-letter.org/Jul2023 (PDF, 11 pages, about 2,670 words extracted, including headings).
- Salutation, then the title line: "Joint statement of scientists and researchers on EU's proposed Child Sexual Abuse Regulation: 4 July 2023".
- Opening substantive sentence: "The signatories of this statement are scientists and researchers from across the globe."
- Second sentence, before any objection: "First and foremost, we acknowledge that child sexual abuse and exploitation is a very serious crime which can cause lifelong harm to survivors."
- Sentence carrying the ask: "As the problems we describe speak to measures that are at the core of the EU's legislative proposal, it is our professional recommendation as scientists that such a proposal be not taken forward."
- Closing warning: "We therefore strongly warn against pursuing these or similar measures as their success is not possible given current and foreseeable technology, while their potential for harm is substantial."
- Structure: about one page of framing, then numbered technical sections (the first is that detection technologies are deeply flawed and vulnerable to attacks). It also says the law "undermines the thoughtful and incisive work that European researchers have provided in cybersecurity and privacy", i.e. it appeals to the signers' own field as a stake.
- Not found: signature count inside this PDF.

### 6.2 EU scientists' letter, 9 September 2025 (primary text read; the PDF carries the final tally)
Source: https://csa-scientist-open-letter.org/Sep2025 (PDF, about 7,900 words including the signatory list, so the body is shorter; I did not isolate its length).
- The PDF header states: "Signatures on Oct 12 2025 (SIGNATURES CLOSED) Signatories: 807 Countries: 37". This supersedes "nearly 500 from 34 countries" in the table (that was the figure in September coverage). Entry criterion stated: "PhD or demonstrated research track record required". The document lists named press contacts in 17 countries, mostly senior academic security and cryptography researchers. Organiser: not named as an organisation; the press contacts are individual professors.
- Header note on events: "On October 8th, the Member States failed to find an agreement and the planned vote to approve the current version of the chat control regulation has been cancelled".
- Opening sentence: "We are writing in response to the new proposal by the Presidency dated 24 July 2025." Next: "We share your concerns about the abuse of children in hideous crimes, resulting in serious harms to the victims and their families."
- It credits the policy-makers: "we are pleased to note the improvements in the new draft of the regulation proposal including the incorporation of some of the recommendations in our letters of July 2023, May 2024, and September 2024."
- The objection sentence: "However, we read in dismay how none of the changes address our major concerns: it is simply not feasible to perform detection of known and new CSAM for hundreds of millions of users with an acceptable level of accuracy, independently of the specific filter."
- The ask in this version is less one sentence than a recommendation carried by two passages: to call attention to measures "recommended by organisations such as the UN" (education, trauma-sensitive reporting hotlines, keyword-search based interventions) and "we reiterate our recommendation to substantially increase investment and effort in supporting proven approaches towards eradicating abuse."
- The letter also complains about process: "policy makers have failed to create an open dialogue with experts on this topic in the last two years."
- Lesson visible in the series: each version thanks the legislators for what they adopted before restating what remains unacceptable.

### 6.3 Apple scanning open letter, 6 August 2021 (secondary access only)
Source for text: https://macdailynews.com/2021/08/09/open-letter-against-apples-privacy-invasive-content-scanning-tech-gathers-thousands-of-signatures/amp/ (repost). The original site is now a domain-for-sale page; I could not read it.
- Its title as quoted in search results: "Security & Privacy Experts, Cryptographers, Researchers, Professors, Legal Experts and Apple Consumers Decry Apple's Planned Move to Undermine User Privacy and End-to-End Encryption" (secondary; treat as near-exact).
- First substantive sentence (per the fetch tool, quoted in fragments): "On August 5th, 2021, Apple Inc. announced new technological measures meant to apply across virtually all of its devices" ... "under the umbrella of 'Expanded Protections for Children'."
- Ask, under the heading "Our Request": "Apple Inc.'s deployment of its proposed content monitoring technology is halted immediately." and "Apple Inc. issue a statement reaffirming their commitment to end-to-end encryption and to user privacy."
- Form: two numbered demands, each aimed at one named company; the second is a cheap, checkable public statement. The CDT-organised coalition letter of 19 August 2021 was a separate document (https://cdt.org/insights/international-coalition-calls-on-apple-to-abandon-plan-to-build-surveillance-capabilities-into-iphones-ipads-and-other-products/); I did not read its text. The search summary credited the CDT with organising the coalition letter and the open letter of 6 August is a different grassroots letter; one search summary conflated them, so treat attribution of the 6 August letter's organiser as unconfirmed.

### 6.5 Apple v. FBI, 2016: amicus briefs by cryptographers and security researchers (search only)
- EFF filed a brief on behalf of 46 technologists, security researchers and cryptographers; Hellman and Rivest are named among them (search result summarising https://www.eff.org/press/releases/eff-and-46-technology-experts-ask-court-throw-out-unconstitutional-apple-order). Its argument, per EFF's own page title and search summary, was that compelling Apple to write and sign code violates the First Amendment.
- A separate Stanford CIS brief for iPhone security experts and applied cryptographers listed signers including Schneier, Shacham and (per another CIS item) Boneh (https://cyberlaw.stanford.edu/blog/2016/03/cis-files-amici-curiae-brief-apple-case-behalf-iphone-security-experts-and-applied/).
- Lesson (my reading): in this setting the cryptographers' ask was framed as a legal argument and the signatories were experts for a court, not petitioners; the format (amicus) already fixes length and tone. The case was dropped by the government after the FBI obtained access by another route (memory; NOT CHECKED).
- NOT read: the briefs themselves.

### 6.6 Australia, Assistance and Access Bill 2018 (search only)
- No single cryptographer open letter found. There were individual and institutional submissions to the Home Affairs consultation: Culnane and Teague (University of Melbourne), the MIT Internet Policy Research Initiative, and others (list at https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/how-to-engage-us-subsite/files/assistance-access-bill-2018/). A letter to the parliamentary committee dated 14 November 2018 on technical capability notices exists; its signatories were not shown in the snippet. Culnane and Teague's own submission welcomed the split between access and capability notices while warning a capability notice could weaken security, and recommended limiting assistance notices to information the provider already holds (search summary). 
- Lesson (my reading): this is a "consultation submission" genre: technical, amendment-oriented, and addressed to a specific clause, not a refusal of the whole bill.
- Outcome: the Act passed in December 2018 (memory; NOT CHECKED).
