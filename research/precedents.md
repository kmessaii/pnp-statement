# Precedents: when research was restrained, paused, renounced, redacted or stopped

Compiled 2026-11-23 from public sources. This file is history only. It makes no argument for or against restraining any line of research.

**How to read the sourcing.** Every claim carries a URL. Where I pulled a document's full text and quoted from it directly, the quotation is marked **[text checked]**. Other quotations came through a page-reading tool that gives back short excerpts. I believe they are exact, but they should be checked against the page before anything is published. Anything I could not find a source for, or know only from memory, is labelled as such in the text and listed again under NOT CHECKED at the end.

## 2. The cases

### 1. Szilard's attempt to delay fission-neutron results, 1939 (failed)

After fission was discovered in January 1939, Leo Szilard tried to get physicists in the US, Britain, France and Denmark to hold back results on neutron emission. The aim was to keep chain-reaction knowledge away from Nazi Germany. On 2 February 1939 he wrote to Frédéric Joliot in Paris. Fermi's first reply to him was "Nuts!" [text checked] ([Weart 1976, PDF](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v29.i2.23_1.online.pdf)).

The Columbia group sent its papers to *Physical Review* in March and agreed to delay their printing. Blackett and Cockcroft agreed in Britain, and Bohr agreed to go along ([Weart](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v29.i2.23_1.online.pdf); [Smyth Report, ch. III](https://www.atomicarchive.com/resources/documents/smyth-report/smyth_iii.html)). Joliot's team had already published in *Nature* that fission releases neutrons. On 7 April they sent *Nature* an estimate of 3 to 4 neutrons per fission. Their cable to Weisskopf read: "QUESTION STUDIED MY OPINION IS TO PUBLISH NOW REGARDS JOLIOT" [text checked] ([Weart](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v29.i2.23_1.online.pdf)).

Weart gives the French reasons. Joliot believed in the international fellowship of scientists. His team feared being eclipsed and losing funding. They doubted that everyone would keep "an unprecedented pact, a pact pushed forward, so far as they knew, only by two Central European refugees". A news release had also made it look as if the facts were already leaking in America. Weart adds: "(Had Fermi, Bohr or a leading American scientist written them about the scheme, the French might have found it more plausible.)" Publication of the French result, he writes, helped start official programmes in Britain, Germany and perhaps the USSR. "The effort of Szilard and his friends, after coming within an inch of success, had failed disastrously." [text checked]

**Primary documents.** Szilard to Joliot, 2 Feb 1939 (Szilard papers, quoted in Weart). S. R. Weart, "Scientists with a secret," *Physics Today* 29(2):23 (Feb 1976), [landing page](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/scientists-with-a-secret).

### 2. The 1940 Reference Committee: voluntary prepublication review (held)

A year later the same aim succeeded. Szilard withheld his own February 1940 reactor paper and persuaded Herbert Anderson to hold back his thesis. Pegram eventually asked Fermi not to publish the carbon absorption cross-section. Louis Turner's paper predicting what became plutonium was delayed. In June 1940 *Physical Review* printed McMillan and Abelson on neptunium, which drew protests, including from Britain ([Weart](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v29.i2.23_1.online.pdf)).

Gregory Breit, sitting on a National Research Council committee, then wrote to journal editors with a plan. Fission papers would be sent to his committee. Sensitive ones would circulate only to a limited group. They would be published later "with a statement of the original date of the paper and with a suitable acknowledgment of the public spirit of the authors" [text checked]. Lawrence wrote to Breit: "As recently as six months ago, I should have been opposed to any such procedure, but I feel now that we are in many respects essentially on a war basis." [text checked] ([Weart](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v29.i2.23_1.online.pdf)).

The Smyth Report says the arrangement "was very successful in preventing publication" and "was a purely voluntary one" ([Smyth Report ¶3.3](https://www.atomicarchive.com/resources/documents/smyth-report/smyth_iii.html)). Weart argues that German scientists later reached a wrong value for carbon and turned away from graphite, so the withheld number may have mattered. That claim is a counterfactual. His verdict: "if self-censorship is difficult, under sufficiently deadly circumstances it can be achieved, and that if it may seem to come late, late may be far better than never." He also notes that it "was imposed against the grain". Szilard and Teller "would later argue strongly for the advantages of open publication." [text checked]

### 3. Norbert Wiener's public renunciation, 1947

In *The Atlantic Monthly* (January 1947), under the title "A Scientist Rebels", Wiener published his refusal to send an aircraft-company researcher a wartime paper on controlled missiles ([text as reprinted](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~rogaway/classes/188/materials/wiener1.pdf)).

He wrote: "I realize, of course, that I am acting as the censor of my own ideas, and it may sound arbitrary, but I will not accept a censorship in which I do not participate." And: "I do not expect to publish any future work of mine which may do damage in the hands of irresponsible militarists." He saw the limit himself: "Since it is obvious that with sufficient effort you can obtain my material, even though it is out of print, I can only protest pro forma in refusing to give you any information concerning my past work." [text checked] ([same](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~rogaway/classes/188/materials/wiener1.pdf))

This is one person withholding his own work, with no mechanism for anyone else. I found no evidence that it slowed missile research. Its effect was on the ethics of the profession. A follow-up essay, "A Rebellious Scientist After Two Years", is reported to have appeared in the *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists* in 1948. I did not check that essay ([secondary mention](https://www.sciencefriday.com/articles/the-rebellious-scientist-who-inspired-kurt-vonnegut/)).

### 4. The Berg letter (1974) and Asilomar (1975)

In July 1974, eleven leading molecular biologists, writing as a National Academy committee, asked scientists worldwide to join them "in voluntarily deferring" two types of experiments. They also called for an international meeting. The letter says "our concern is based on judgments of potential rather than demonstrated risk" and admits that compliance "will entail postponement or possibly abandonment of certain types of scientifically worthwhile experiments" [text checked] (P. Berg et al., "Potential Biohazards of Recombinant DNA Molecules," *Science* 185:303, 26 July 1974. [PDF](https://bio.as.uky.edu/sites/default/files/Original%20Paul%20Berg%20Caution%20on%20Recombinant%20DNA.pdf); [JSTOR](http://www.jstor.org/stable/1738673)).

The Asilomar conference met 24 to 27 February 1975. It ended the blanket pause and replaced it with containment matched to risk. It also held "that there are certain experiments in which the potential risks are of such a serious nature that they ought not to be done with presently available containment facilities". It noted that "future research and experience may show that many of the potential biohazards are less serious and/or less probable than we now suspect" [text checked from OCR] (Berg, Baltimore, Brenner, Roblin, Singer, summary statement, *Science* 188:991, 6 June 1975, [scan](https://ipmall.law.unh.edu/sites/default/files/BAYHDOLE/bremmerPDF/Asilomar_Conference_on_Recombinant_DNA_Molecules,_6-6-1975.pdf); also *PNAS* 72:1981, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC432675)). NIH guidelines followed in 1976.

The National Library of Medicine calls the moratorium "the first in the history of science" and says it was "universally observed". It adds that experience "showed that the dangers were minimal" ([NLM](https://profiles.nlm.nih.gov/spotlight/cd/feature/dna)). There was also local politics. Cambridge, Massachusetts imposed its own moratorium in 1976 and passed an ordinance in 1977 ([Crimson 1976](https://thecrimson.com/article/1976/9/30/council-extends-dna-experiment-ban-wald); [Crimson 1977](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/1977/2/8/city-council-approves-dna-research-pthe)). In a 2004 essay Berg doubted that the model transfers to later controversies ([Nobel site](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1980/berg/article/); I could not open it, see NOT CHECKED).

### 5. GCHQ's secret invention of public-key cryptography, 1970 to 1997

James Ellis described "non-secret encryption" in a classified CESG report in January 1970. Clifford Cocks wrote "A Note on Non-Secret Encryption" (20 Nov 1973), which Ellis calls "essentially the RSA Algorithm". Malcolm Williamson wrote "Non-Secret Encryption Using a Finite Field" (21 Jan 1974), a key-exchange scheme Ellis calls identical to Diffie-Hellman's [text checked] (J. H. Ellis, "The history of Non-Secret Encryption," [PDF](https://www.cryptocellar.org/cesg/ellis.pdf)).

Diffie and Hellman published in 1976 and Rivest, Shamir and Adleman in 1977 to 1978. GCHQ announced its priority in December 1997, a month after Ellis died ([GCHQ](https://www.gchq.gov.uk/person/james-ellis); [NSA biography](https://nsa.gov/Press-Room/Digital-Media-Center/Biographies/Biography-View-Page/Article/3006218/clifford-cocks-james-ellis-and-malcolm-williamson)).

Ellis explains the norm: "Most professional scientists aim to be the first to publish their work, because it is through dissemination that the work realises its value. In contrast, the fullest value of cryptography is realised by minimising the information available to potential adversaries." Secrets are released only "after it has been demonstrated clearly that no further benefit can be obtained from continued secrecy." [text checked] ([Ellis](https://www.cryptocellar.org/cesg/ellis.pdf))

The secrecy held for 27 years. It did not keep the knowledge exclusive: the open community independently re-found it six years after Ellis's first report. A declassified NSA history, quoted in *Stanford Magazine*, says: "NSA regarded the [Diffie-Hellman] technique as classified. Now it was out in the open." [text checked] ([Corrigan-Gibbs 2014](https://stanfordmag.org/contents/keeping-secrets)) Schneier's view is that GCHQ "never understood its significance" ([Schneier 1998](https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/1998/04/the_secret_story_of.html)). That is a judgement, not a finding.

### 6. Differential cryptanalysis kept quiet by IBM, about 1974 to 1990

IBM's DES design team knew differential cryptanalysis by 1974 and designed the S-boxes against it. In 1994 Don Coppersmith of IBM explained what happened: "After discussions with NSA, it was decided that disclosure of the design considerations would reveal the technique of differential cryptanalysis, a powerful technique that can be used against many ciphers. This in turn would weaken the competitive advantage the United States enjoyed over other countries in the field of cryptography." Elsewhere in the paper he writes that "there was concern that placing such information in the public domain could adversely affect national security." [text checked; the two-column scan was rejoined by hand] (D. Coppersmith, "The Data Encryption Standard (DES) and its strength against attacks," *IBM J. Res. Dev.* 38(3):243–250, May 1994, [PDF](https://people.clarkson.edu/class/cs456/CoppersmithDES.pdf); [dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/ibmrd/Coppersmith94.html)).

The secret held about 16 years and ended through independent rediscovery. Coppersmith traces open precursors to den Boer (1988) and Murphy (1990), and then the papers of Biham and Shamir. Shamir had demonstrated an attack on 8-round DES at Securicom in 1989 "without making the techniques known" [text checked] ([same](https://people.clarkson.edu/class/cs456/CoppersmithDES.pdf)).

Secrecy had a cost in trust: "Many people speculated, however, that the lack of disclosure was due to some 'trap door' or hidden weakness in the DES. One of the purposes of the present paper is to dispel this notion" [text checked]. Separately, a declassified NSA history says NSA pressed for a shorter key, and the design settled on 56 bits ([Corrigan-Gibbs 2014](https://stanfordmag.org/contents/keeping-secrets)).

### 7. The "Meyer letter" to the IEEE, 1977 (failed)

In July 1977 J. A. Meyer, an IEEE member who turned out to work for the NSA, wrote to the IEEE. He warned that cryptography papers planned for the October 1977 International Symposium on Information Theory at Cornell might break the International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR). In the words of the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) account, they "might subject the authors and the IEEE to prosecution" ([NAS 1982, Appendix E](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/253/chapter/14)).

Martin Hellman asked Stanford's counsel for advice. Counsel's informal advice, as Hellman recalled it: "If you are prosecuted, Stanford will defend you. But if you're found guilty, we can't pay your fine and we can't go to jail for you." His students Pohlig and Merkle stood on stage while Hellman presented their work [text checked] (H. Corrigan-Gibbs, "Keeping Secrets," *Stanford Magazine*, Nov/Dec 2014, [web](https://stanfordmag.org/contents/keeping-secrets), [PDF](http://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~henrycg/files/academic/papers/stanfordmag14keeping.pdf)). The symposium went ahead and "no action was taken by the government" ([NAS](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/253/chapter/14)). NSA director Inman later cited a Senate committee finding that the letter was a personal initiative ([NAS](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/253/chapter/14)).

Hellman's later reflection: "Forget about what's right. Go with this, you've got a tiger by the tail. You'll never have more of an impact on society." Also: "Thinking it through even now, I still would have done most of what I did. But it could have been something as bad as inventing nuclear weapons, and so I vowed I would never do that again." Inman's later view was that "there was no demand" for the encryption that became available [text checked] ([Corrigan-Gibbs](http://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~henrycg/files/academic/papers/stanfordmag14keeping.pdf)).

### 8. Invention Secrecy Act orders on cryptography, 1978 and 1987 (each reversed)

In October 1977 the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee filed a patent application for an encryption device invented by George Davida. About six months later the Patent Office imposed a secrecy order, warning of "a $10,000 fine and two years in prison". The order was rescinded in June 1978. A similar order on a voice scrambler filed by three Seattle engineers was lifted that October ([NAS 1982, Appendix E](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/253/chapter/14)). Landau reports that the Davida order came at NSA's request and was explained as a "bureaucratic snafu" [text checked] ([Landau 1988](https://www.privacyink.org/pdf/Zero_Knowledge.pdf)).

On 6 January 1987, at the Army's request, a secrecy order was imposed on Feige, Fiat and Shamir's patent application for zero-knowledge identification (filed 9 July 1986). It said disclosure "would be detrimental to the national security". The work had already been presented at the International Congress of Mathematicians and at CRYPTO '86. "Within two days the secrecy order was rescinded." Shamir later thanked "the NSA guys" who "were extremely helpful behind the scenes in removing the order" [text checked] (S. Landau, "Zero Knowledge and the Department of Defense," *Notices of the AMS* 35(1), Jan 1988, [PDF](https://www.privacyink.org/pdf/Zero_Knowledge.pdf)).

Landau notes that the Patent Office was then issuing "about 350 new secrecy orders annually" [text checked]. In all three cases the order failed on contact with publicity, and in the last one the knowledge was already public.

### 9. NSA's voluntary prepublication review and the Public Cryptography Study Group, 1980s

NSA director Bobby Inman told academics that open publication harmed NSA's mission, and warned that the agency might seek legislation ([Landau](https://www.privacyink.org/pdf/Zero_Knowledge.pdf)). The NSA history says his draft bill was "dead on arrival" [text checked] ([Corrigan-Gibbs](http://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~henrycg/files/academic/papers/stanfordmag14keeping.pdf)). The American Council on Education convened the Public Cryptography Study Group in 1980 with NSF funding. It rejected mandatory review and recommended a trial voluntary scheme. Authors would send papers to NSA when they submitted to journals, with appeal to a committee of two NSA appointees and three appointed by the President's science adviser: "The entire process would be voluntary" ([NAS 1982, Appendix E](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/253/chapter/14)).

George Davida dissented. He warned that it "could be a first step toward a compulsory system" ([NAS](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/253/chapter/14)) and called NSA's efforts "unnecessary, divisive, wasteful" [text checked] ([Landau](https://www.privacyink.org/pdf/Zero_Knowledge.pdf)).

On results, the sources give different counts. The NAS says about 46 papers were handled and two had security implications, both "resolved to all parties' satisfaction" ([NAS](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/253/chapter/14)). Landau says 35 papers by 1982 with changes asked in two, over 500 by 1988, and "On at least one occasion, a researcher did not publish work because of an NSA request." [text checked] ([Landau](https://www.privacyink.org/pdf/Zero_Knowledge.pdf)). Inman later said the scheme ran for about a decade and "fell apart" because of "the explosion of . . . uses" [text checked] ([Corrigan-Gibbs](http://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~henrycg/files/academic/papers/stanfordmag14keeping.pdf)).

Funding was a second route. After Inman wrote to NSF in 1980 about proposals by Adleman and Rivest, NSF approved Adleman's grant (9 December 1980) on terms that let NSF review results before publication. MIT objected to that wording, and Rivest's grant was made on 25 September 1981 with revised language ([NAS](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/253/chapter/14)).

### 10. *United States v. The Progressive*, 1979 (failed)

The government obtained a temporary restraining order on 9 March 1979 and a preliminary injunction on 26 March. They stopped *The Progressive* from publishing Howard Morland's article on the H-bomb, which Morland had compiled from public sources ([First Amendment Encyclopedia](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/united-states-v-progressive-inc-w-d-wis/); 467 F. Supp. 990, [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/467/990/1376343/)). Judge Warren wrote: "A mistake in ruling against the United States could pave the way for thermonuclear annihilation for us all." ([MTSU](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/united-states-v-progressive-inc-w-d-wis/))

Charles Hansen, a private citizen, sent Senator Percy a letter summarizing similar material. The Madison *Press Connection* printed it in full, and the Justice Department dropped the case the next day ([Harvard Crimson, 18 Sep 1979](https://thecrimson.com/article/1979/9/18/government-gives-up-efforts-to-suppress)). The article ran in November 1979 as "The H-Bomb Secret: How We Got It, Why We're Telling It" ([The Progressive](https://www.progressive.org/hbomb)).

This is the leading case where the state treated independently derived knowledge as secret, and the restraint failed because others could derive the same thing.

### 11. The SDI non-participation pledge, 1985 to 1987

Starting at Cornell and the University of Illinois in 1985, researchers pledged "to neither solicit nor accept funding from the Strategic Defense Initiative Organization (SDIO)". By early 1987 the organiser John Kogut reported "nearly 7,000 research scientists" in more than 110 departments, including more than 3,800 senior faculty and nearly 60 percent of the combined faculty of the top 20 US physics departments (J. Kogut, "Say No to a 'Dumb, Dangerous' Program," *The Scientist*, Feb 1987, [web](https://www.the-scientist.com/say-no-to-a-dumb-dangerous-program-63992)). Critics called the boycott ill-advised ([Christian Science Monitor, 20 Sep 1985](https://csmonitor.com/1985/0920/eprof.html)).

I found no measurement of its effect on SDI research. Mathematicians took part: one search summary puts 25 of 42 Princeton mathematics faculty among the signers in October 1985. I could not open that source (see NOT CHECKED).

### 12. Export controls on cryptographic software, to 2000

US export rules treated cryptographic software as munitions, later as dual-use goods. Philip Zimmermann faced a multi-year criminal investigation over PGP spreading abroad. It was dropped on 11 January 1996 with no stated reason ([Zimmermann](https://www.mit.edu/~prz/EN/news/PRZ_case_dropped.html); [IEEE Cipher](https://www.ieee-security.org/Cipher/Newsbriefs/1996/960214.zimmerman.html)).

In *Bernstein v. US Department of Justice* (9th Cir., 6 May 1999) the majority said the government "is intentionally retarding the progress of the flourishing science of cryptography" and that such efforts "would appear to strike deep into the heartland of the First Amendment" ([opinion](https://www.eff.org/files/filenode/bernstein/19990506_circuit_decision.html)). That opinion was withdrawn for rehearing en banc. The case was then mooted when the rules changed ([Columbia Global Freedom of Expression](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/bernstein-v-department-of-justice/)). The 14 January 2000 rule allowed export of publicly available encryption source code after notifying the government ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2000-01-14/html/00-983.htm)). The control failed because publication and the internet made "export" impossible to contain.

### 13. Felten and the SDMI paper, 2001 (failed)

Edward Felten's team broke the watermarking schemes in the music industry's public SDMI challenge. On 9 April 2001 a letter on SDMI/RIAA letterhead warned that publication "could subject you and you research team [sic] to actions under the Digital Millennium Copyright Act" and urged them "to withdraw the paper" from the Information Hiding Workshop ([letter](https://w2.eff.org/IP/DMCA/Felten_v_RIAA/20010409_riaa_sdmi_letter.html)). The authors pulled it on 25 or 26 April; EFF's timeline lists both dates. The paper was accepted for USENIX Security (13 to 17 August 2001), and the authors sued for a declaratory judgment on 6 June 2001 ([EFF timeline](https://w2.eff.org/IP/DMCA/Felten_v_RIAA/faq_felten.html); [EFF case page](https://www.eff.org/cases/felten-et-al-v-riaa-et-al)).

This was a restraint imposed by a private party with a direct interest. The publicity it drew ensured the paper reached a wider audience.

### 14. The journal editors' statement on publication and security, 2003

On 15 February 2003, editors of major journals announced in *Science*, *Nature* and *PNAS* that "an editor may conclude that the potential harm of publication outweighs the potential societal benefits," in which case the paper "should be modified, or not be published" ([statement](https://sgp.fas.org/news/2003/02/sci021503.html)). Stanley Falkow's *PNAS* editorial was titled "'Statement on scientific publication and security' fails to provide necessary guidelines" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC156238)).

I found no documented case of a paper refused under the statement. The H5N1 dispute in 2011 to 2012 (next case) tested it, and both papers were published in full. Mechanism: editorial discretion with no external body. Whether it worked is unknown.

### 15. The NSABB recommendation to redact the H5N1 papers, 2011 to 2012 (reversed)

Two groups, led by Fouchier and by Kawaoka, made H5N1 viruses transmissible between ferrets. On 20 December 2011 the US National Science Advisory Board for Biosecurity (NSABB) recommended publishing the conclusions but not "the methodological and other details that could enable replication of the experiments." HHS passed this to the authors and editors as non-binding. Board chair Paul Keim said: "This is not censorship, this is a request to postpone [publication] so there can be a broader discussion." ([CIDRAP, 20 Dec 2011](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/cidrap/content/influenza/avianflu/news/dec2011nsabb.html))

*Science* made its compliance conditional on a written plan for sharing the omitted details with responsible researchers. *Nature*'s editor said it was "essential for public health" that full details be available ([same](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/cidrap/content/influenza/avianflu/news/dec2011nsabb.html)). No such plan was produced. On 30 March 2012 the NSABB reversed itself: unanimous for the revised Kawaoka paper and 12 to 6 for Fouchier's ([CIDRAP, Apr 2012](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/cidrap/content/influenza/avianflu/news/apr2012nih.html); [NAS summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18255/chapter/2)). Kawaoka's paper appeared in *Nature* on 2 May 2012, and Fouchier's in *Science* in June 2012 ([CIDRAP](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/cidrap/content/influenza/avianflu/news/may0312kawaoka.html)).

### 16. The researchers' H5N1 pause, 2012 to 2013, and the Dutch export permit

On 20 January 2012, 39 influenza researchers announced in *Science* and *Nature* "a voluntary pause of 60 days on any research involving highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 viruses" leading to more transmissible viruses. The reason given was that "organizations and governments around the world need time to find the best solutions" (R. Fouchier et al., "Pause on avian flu transmission research," *Science* 335:400 and *Nature* 481:443, [CIDRAP report](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/cidrap/content/influenza/avianflu/news/jan2012pause.html)).

The pause lasted about a year. It ended with "Transmission studies resume for avian flu" (*Nature* 493:609 and *Science* 339:520, January 2013, [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11858)). Kawaoka said: "The risk exists in nature already, and not doing the research is really putting us in danger." Tom Inglesby: "I don't think they should resume. I think the moratorium should go on." ([NPR/GBH, 23 Jan 2013](https://www.wgbh.org/news/2013-01-23/scientists-put-an-end-to-moratorium-on-bird-flu-research)) Fouchier said all 40 signers had agreed to restart ([same](https://www.wgbh.org/news/2013-01-23/scientists-put-an-end-to-moratorium-on-bird-flu-research)), so the counts differ: 39 at the start, 40 at the end.

Separately, the Netherlands required Fouchier to obtain an EU dual-use export licence before submitting his paper. He got it under protest in April 2012. A district court upheld the requirement in September 2013. An appeals court annulled that ruling in 2015, and sources describe its grounds differently ([CIDRAP](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/avian-influenza-bird-flu/dutch-court-affirms-limit-publishing-h5n1-findings); [Nature blog](https://blogs.nature.com/blog/court-upholds-need-for-export-permits-for-risky-flu-research/)).

### 17. The US gain-of-function funding pause, 2014 to 2017

On 17 October 2014, after laboratory accidents at CDC ([NPR/GBH](https://www.wgbh.org/news/2014-10-24/scientists-fight-for-superbug-research-as-u-s-pauses-funding)), the US paused new funding for "research projects that reasonably may be anticipated to confer attributes to influenza, MERS, or SARS viruses such that the resulting virus has enhanced pathogenicity and/or transmissibility (via the respiratory route) in mammals." The pause was "to assess the potential benefits and risks", with a waiver where work was "urgently necessary to protect public health or national security" ([NIH NOT-OD-15-011](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-15-011.html)).

The reaction split. Marc Lipsitch: "I think the moratorium is long over-due". Arturo Casadevall: "a knee-jerk reaction to a very complex problem" ([NPR/GBH](https://www.wgbh.org/news/2014-10-24/scientists-fight-for-superbug-research-as-u-s-pauses-funding)).

It ended on 19 December 2017 with the HHS P3CO review framework ([NOT-OD-17-071](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-17-071.html)). The *New York Times* reported that it halted 21 projects and that NIH later exempted 10. Collins: "We see this as a rigorous policy". Lipsitch on the new review: "a small step forward" ([NYT via WRAL, 19 Dec 2017](https://www.wral.com/a-federal-ban-on-making-lethal-viruses-is-lifted/17199594/)).

The line was redrawn later. Executive Order 14292 (May 2025) led to a July 2026 federal policy that stops funding for "dangerous gain-of-function" research ([NOT-OD-26-101](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-26-101.html); [policy PDF](https://www.aspr.gov/sites/default/files/2026-07/Stopping-High-Risk-Life-Sciences-Research-Policy-508.pdf)).

### 18. Human reproductive cloning, 1998 onward

The Council of Europe's Additional Protocol on the Prohibition of Cloning Human Beings (ETS 168, opened 12 January 1998) prohibits any intervention seeking to create a human being genetically identical to another, "whether living or dead" ([text](https://rm.coe.int/168007f2ca); [UMN copy](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/instree/cloningconv.html)). On 8 March 2005 the UN General Assembly adopted the non-binding UN Declaration on Human Cloning (resolution 59/280) by 84 to 34 with 37 abstentions ([UN Digital Library](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/543570); [UN press](https://press.un.org/en/2005/gal3271.doc.htm)). The split was mostly over whether to ban research cloning as well.

The core restraint on reproductive cloning has held for roughly 28 years, measured from 1998. I found no credible report of a cloned human. From memory and not checked here: unverified claims by the group Clonaid in 2002, the birth of cloned macaques reported in *Cell* in 2018, and the US having no federal statute. The professional consensus and national laws, rather than the declaration, appear to carry the weight.

### 19. Heritable human genome editing, 2015 onward

The December 2015 international summit, co-hosted by the US, UK and Chinese academies, said: "It would be irresponsible to proceed with any clinical use of germline editing unless and until" safety issues were resolved and there was broad societal consensus. It added that the question "should be revisited on a regular basis" ([statement](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2015/12/on-human-gene-editing-international-summit-statement)). In November 2018 He Jiankui announced edited twins. The organisers said that proceeding "remains irresponsible" ([2018 statement](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2018/11/statement-by-the-organizing-committee-of-the-second-international-summit-on-human-genome-editing)). A Shenzhen court sentenced him to three years for illegal medical practice on 30 December 2019 ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2019/12/30/792340177/chinese-researcher-who-created-gene-edited-babies-sentenced-to-3-years-in-prison)).

In March 2019 Lander and 17 others called for a fixed-term global moratorium, which NIH backed. Doudna doubted that it would stop determined scientists ([Nature comment](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-019-00726-5); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2019/03/13/701549223/call-for-global-moratorium-on-creating-gene-edited-babies)). The 2023 summit: "Heritable human genome editing remains unacceptable at this time." ([Royal Society](https://royalsociety.org/news/2023/03/statement-third-international-summit-human-genome-editing/))

The line was broken once and punished. In 2025, startups including Preventive, reported to have raised $30 million, began studying heritable editing, looking at jurisdictions outside the US ([NPR, Aug 2025](https://www.npr.org/sections/shots-health-news/2025/08/06/nx-s1-5493448/gene-editing-human-embryos-designer-babies); [Business Standard, Nov 2025](https://www.business-standard.com/health/sam-altman-brian-armstrong-embryo-gene-editing-preventive-startup-125110900170_1.html)).

### 20. Solar geoengineering field experiments: SPICE, SCoPEx, Mexico, the non-use call

**SPICE (UK).** The team cancelled its 1 km balloon-and-hose test in 2012. The funder had not cleared a public-engagement "stage gate", and team members had filed a related patent. Hugh Hunt: "It's their money so we can't spend it on something they've expressly told us not to do until we get the green light." Peter Cox: "research projects such as SPICE need to be squeaky clean" ([Physics World, 22 May 2012](https://physicsworld.com/a/geoengineering-balloon-trial-cancelled/)).

**SCoPEx (Harvard).** Harvard's advisory committee recommended postponing a 2021 test flight in Sweden pending public engagement. The Saami Council opposed it, and the Swedish Space Corporation cancelled the flight ([phys.org, Apr 2021](https://phys.org/news/2021-04-controversial-flight-aimed-cooling-planet.html)). On 18 March 2024 the principal investigator said he was "no longer pursuing the experiment" ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2024/03/18/1089879/harvard-halts-its-long-planned-atmospheric-geoengineering-experiment/)).

**The non-use call.** In January 2022 more than 60 scholars called for an International Non-Use Agreement covering public funding, outdoor experiments, patents and deployment (F. Biermann et al., *WIREs Climate Change* e754, [Utrecht](https://www.uu.nl/en/news/call-for-non-use-agreement-solar-geoengineering)).

**The limit.** A startup, Make Sunsets, launched sulfur-dioxide balloons in Mexico without sanction. On 13 January 2023 Mexico announced it would prohibit such experiments ([Time](https://time.com/6248654/mexico-geoengineering-ban-make-sunsets)). Field work is cheap, so outsiders can do it.

### 21. GPT-2's staged release, 2019

On 14 February 2019 OpenAI wrote: "Due to our concerns about malicious applications of the technology, we are not releasing the trained model." ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/better-language-models/)) Larger versions were released in stages. On 22 August 2019 four Brown researchers released a replication of the 1.5-billion-parameter model, estimating about $50k of compute. They argued that "replication efforts are not unique" ([OpenGPT-2, mirror](https://gwern.net/docs/www/medium.com/7cf2e5b2877d65b5629d6a12b67afb87e05898f7.html)). OpenAI's August 2019 report says another replicator, Connor Leahy, changed his mind about releasing after discussion ([Solaiman et al., arXiv 1908.09203](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/1908.09203)).

OpenAI released the full model on 5 November 2019: "We've seen no strong evidence of misuse so far." ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/gpt-2-1-5b-release/)) This was a temporary restraint with a planned exit. It was overtaken in months because the work was cheap to reproduce.

### 22. Horsepox synthesis, 2017 to 2018 (restraint asked for, not given)

Noyce, Lederman and Evans reported making an infectious horsepox virus from synthetic DNA, as a vaccine candidate, in *PLOS ONE* (19 January 2018) ([DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/688cffea42b74d49b897133d5b338292)). Critics, concerned about the relevance to smallpox, wrote to journals urging them not to publish. *PLOS ONE* published anyway ([Ed Yong, *The Atlantic*, 4 Oct 2018, via MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/articles/a-controversial-virus-study-reveals-a-critical-flaw-in-how-science-is-done/)). *PLOS Pathogens* then ran opposing opinion pieces, one by Inglesby ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6171949)).

There was no moratorium, no binding review and no redaction. The decision rested with one journal, after a national biosafety approval of the lab work itself. I kept this case short on purpose. Two governance-only articles about it could not be retrieved through my tools (a content filter), so its details are thinner than the others' (see NOT CHECKED).

### 23. The FLI open letter on AI training, 2023 (did not achieve its ask)

On 22 March 2023 the Future of Life Institute (FLI) published: "We call on all AI labs to immediately pause for at least 6 months the training of AI systems more powerful than GPT-4." It added: "If such a pause cannot be enacted quickly, governments should step in and institute a moratorium." ([letter](https://futureoflife.org/open-letter/pause-giant-ai-experiments/))

At six months FLI said companies acknowledge "the potential need for a pause" but are "unable or unwilling to say when or even how such a slowdown might occur". It credited the letter with having "kicked off the global conversation about AI risk" ([FLI, 21 Sep 2023](https://futureoflife.org/ai/six-month-letter-expires/)). Its newsletter said that "a pause never materialized - if anything, the 'arms race' between AI corporations has only intensified" ([FLI newsletter, Sep 2023](https://newsletter.futureoflife.org/p/fli-newsletter-september-2023)). By FLI's own account, no lab paused.

### 24. Mirror life, December 2024 onward

On 12 December 2024, 38 scientists wrote in *Science*: "We therefore recommend that research with the goal of creating mirror bacteria not be permitted," and asked funders to "make clear that they will not support such work." They said they "were initially skeptical" and had grown "deeply concerned". They put the capability "at least a decade away by many estimations" ([Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](https://thebulletin.org/2024/12/scientists-urge-halt-to-research-on-creating-synthetic-mirror-bacteria-that-could-evade-human-immunity-disrupt-ecosystems/)) (K. P. Adamala et al., "Confronting risks of mirror life," *Science* 386:1351, issue of 20 Dec 2024, [JCVI record](https://www.jcvi.org/publications/confronting-risks-mirror-life)). A technical report of nearly 300 pages accompanied it ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/creating-mirror-life-could-be-disastrous-scientists-warn/)).

Kate Adamala "previously worked toward creating mirror cells". She said: "All the practical applications that drew us into this field are the reasons we're terrified of it now" ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/creating-mirror-life-could-be-disastrous-scientists-warn/)).

By October 2025, scientists in China, the US and Europe, "including several who previously pursued this goal," agreed mirror bacteria should not be made. Meetings followed in Paris and Manchester. The authors of that update knew of no one pursuing the goal and reported no funder commitments yet ([Carnegie, 3 Oct 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/posts/2025/10/mirror-life-bacteria-risks-prevention)). The US policy of 20 July 2026 says its funding stop "includes creation of mirror organisms" [text checked] ([policy PDF](https://www.aspr.gov/sites/default/files/2026-07/Stopping-High-Risk-Life-Sciences-Research-Policy-508.pdf)).

### 25. SHAttered: coordinated disclosure of a practical SHA-1 collision, 2017

On 23 February 2017 Google and CWI Amsterdam published two different PDFs with the same SHA-1 hash, and a free detection tool. Under Google's disclosure policy they held back the attack code for 90 days ([Google Security Blog](https://security.googleblog.com/2017/02/announcing-first-sha1-collision.html); [TechCrunch archived at CWI](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/26592/TechCrunch-24feb2017-Security-researchers-announce-first-practical-SHA-1-collision-attack.pdf)). The project page notes "theoretical attacks have been known since 2005, and SHA-1 was officially deprecated by NIST in 2011" ([shattered.io mirror](https://marc-stevens.nl/research/shattered.io/)).

This is the cryptographic community's working norm: a short, pre-announced delay with an end date, applied to an exploit rather than to the mathematics, and tied to steps others can take in the meantime. It held as designed. I could not confirm whether, or when, the attack code itself was released.

## 3. What the cases have in common

**This section is my reading of the cases above, not a sourced finding.**

**The successes share these features.**

1. **Few people could do the work, and they could all be reached.** In 1940 a handful of US labs and one journal editor's office could close the gap. The IBM and GCHQ secrets sat inside small closed groups bound by employment or law. Mirror bacteria are a decade away, and the people who might build them are a small, named community. Where the capable set was wide, or the work cheap (GPT-2 at about $50k, sulfur balloons, a magazine writer reading public sources), restraint was overtaken.
2. **The restraint came from insiders with standing.** The Berg letter was signed by the people doing the experiments. The mirror-life authors include people who had worked toward the goal. The 1940 scheme had Fermi, Lawrence and Breit inside it. Weart's account of 1939 turns on the opposite: the French saw a pact pushed "only by two Central European refugees". Restraints imposed from outside by agencies (the Meyer letter, secrecy orders, NSABB's redaction, the Progressive injunction) mostly collapsed, often within weeks, and usually after drawing publicity to the very work they targeted.
3. **They had an exit, or a scheduled replacement.** Asilomar turned a moratorium into graded rules within seven months. The H5N1 pause was defined by what had to happen before it ended. The GOF pause ended in a review framework. SHAttered named its 90 days in advance. The failures were open-ended (FLI) or had no stated criteria for ending (the NSABB redaction, which had no plan for sharing the details).
4. **A concrete alternative was offered, often one that protected credit.** Breit's committee kept priority by dating withheld papers and promising later publication with credit. Szilard even proposed paying young physicists for the sacrifice. Asilomar offered containment levels instead of a ban. The PCSG scheme offered appeal to a mixed panel. Where the only offer was "don't", it held only where no one strongly wanted to proceed (mirror life, so far).
5. **The danger was concrete and shared.** In 1940 it was war. Recombinant DNA had a named mechanism of harm. The mirror-life authors describe a specific failure mode. The AI pause and the 1977 cryptography fights had diffuse or contested harms, and capable people disagreed.

**The failures share these features.**

1. **One capable actor stayed outside.** Examples: Joliot (1939), He Jiankui (2018), Make Sunsets (2022), the Brown replicators (2019), and arguably the 2025 embryo-editing startups. A voluntary restraint is only as strong as its least willing capable member.
2. **The knowledge was already public in part, or could be rebuilt from what was.** Feige-Fiat-Shamir had been presented at the ICM before the order. Morland and Hansen worked from public sources. After *Scientific American* described RSA in August 1977, its authors received 7,000 requests for their technical report, according to Levy as cited by Corrigan-Gibbs ([Corrigan-Gibbs](http://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~henrycg/files/academic/papers/stanfordmag14keeping.pdf)).
3. **Commercial competition and no shared verification.** The FLI letter asked competing firms for a pause that no one could verify. By FLI's own account no lab paused. Heritable editing now faces the same pressure from funded startups.
4. **Secrecy cost trust.** The silence about the DES S-boxes bred two decades of trapdoor suspicion, which Coppersmith had to dispel in 1994. By 2014 Corrigan-Gibbs described trust between academic cryptographers and NSA as "at its nadir" ([Corrigan-Gibbs](http://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~henrycg/files/academic/papers/stanfordmag14keeping.pdf)).

**On mathematics in particular.** The two pure-mathematics secrets on record, public-key cryptography and differential cryptanalysis, both held as *secrets* and both failed as *barriers*. Outsiders independently rediscovered public-key cryptography about 6 years after Ellis (1970 to 1976), and RSA about 4 years after Cocks's equivalent (1973 to 1977). They rediscovered differential cryptanalysis about 16 years after IBM. Neither was rediscovered because anything leaked. The NSA acknowledged that Diffie and Hellman worked without access to classified material ([Corrigan-Gibbs](http://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~henrycg/files/academic/papers/stanfordmag14keeping.pdf)). Both were rediscovered because the problems were natural and well posed and capable people were working near them. In both cases the secret was knowledge already held by a closed group. None of the precedents I found is of a community agreeing not to look for a mathematical result that no one yet has.
