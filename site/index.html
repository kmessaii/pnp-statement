Draft, 25 November 2026

AI labs should stop working on P = NP

Locks only go one way.

Since July, AI systems have

disproved the Jacobian conjecture, 19 July

broken a post-quantum signature scheme, 28 July

solved the Navier–Stokes problem, 8 September

refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis 16 October

and proved the Riemann hypothesis 20 November

and found a fast method for the hardest problems in computing.

Jacobian conjecture, 19 July: false in three dimensions and above.

HAWK, 28 July: the cost of recovering a HAWK-512 key fell from 2150 to 2108. Its designers withdrew it from NIST's third round the next day.

Navier–Stokes, 8 September: blow-up in finite time with smooth forcing, formalised in Lean.

Exponential Time Hypothesis, 16 October: formalised in Lean.

Riemann hypothesis, 20 November: formalised in Lean.

If P = NP

P versus NP asks whether every problem whose answer is quick to check is also quick to solve. Nearly all of modern security assumes it is not. A password, a private key, the signature on a software update: each is an answer that is quick to check and, we trust, hopeless to find.

If P = NP, whoever holds the method can:

  • Read almost any encrypted message.
  • Recover the private key of any bank, any government and any person.
  • Forge the signature on a software update and remotely execute code on any device that accepts updates.
  • Do all of it covertly, because nothing has been broken into.

There is no patch. The new standards built to survive quantum computers are just as vulnerable. Almost nothing is left standing but codes whose secret is as long as the message.

An algorithm solving 3-SAT in n² steps "could be used to factor 200-digit numbers in a few minutes." Cook

"Essentially all the cryptography that we currently use on the Internet" would be broken, the exceptions being "the one-time pad and quantum key distribution". Aaronson

October was the warning shot

The Exponential Time Hypothesis once claimed that the hardest problems are not merely slow to solve, but exponentially slow. If true, P ≠ NP follows. Hundreds of papers were built on it. One month ago it was proven false. Two labs' AI systems did it, with a method far too slow to use.

A proof that P = NP could be just as slow and still be the dangerous one. A proof is all it takes to start what follows:

Too slow: by one analysis its advantage begins near 28.6 × 109 variables.

Only the first step is a choice. After it, everyone knows a fast method is there to be found, and nobody decides who goes looking.

"Once we knew that an algorithm existed, we'd have a massive inducement to try to find it." Aaronson

Warning people first doesn't help

When researchers break cryptographic ciphers, they disclose privately to mitigate the damage. That works because there is another cipher to move to.

There is no other cipher. A warning cannot help when nothing can be changed. It does nothing for traffic already recorded: adversaries collect encrypted data now in the hope of reading it later.

So for this one kind of result, how it is announced matters less than when it is found. Each year it is not found is a year in which banks, software updates and private messages go on working, and a year in which to settle, before the fact, who should be trusted with such a thing. That is what stopping buys.

The SIKE break of 2022 reached its designers about a week before the paper, on 22 and 30 July. Source

"Harvest now, decrypt later": NIST names it as a pressing threat.

What we ask

AI labs should not work on P versus NP. (1)

Frontier labs are the ones solving problems like this. They have the compute and the internal models, and nobody else settled a problem of this size this year. So the ask is made of them: do not aim those systems at P versus NP.

That means not at proving P = NP, and not at proving P ≠ NP. Nobody can aim a system at one answer. A campaign to settle the question returns whichever answer is true.

Each lab should say so in the safety framework it already publishes: for the next two years, no campaigns to resolve P versus NP, and none to speed up algorithms for NP-complete problems, beginning with October's. The commitment holds while everyone keeps it. If any developer is found running such a campaign, the others are released.

The trade is lopsided. If P ≠ NP, as most experts expect, a lab gives up a famous result and nothing else. If P = NP, the lab that finds it first has won nothing: the method opens its own side's locks as surely as anyone else's, and there is no patch to build from it.

If a proof turns up anyway, do not announce it, and do not work on making it fast. The time that buys is for moving what matters most onto keys that depend on no hard problem at all.

This is asked of a few companies and their systems. People have worked on this problem since at least 1971, and nothing here asks them to stop.

We read the published safety frameworks of five labs: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta and xAI. None names a mathematical result as something to hold back. Nor does the Frontier Model Forum's 2026 survey.

One lab has written that "it would be prudent to consider how researchers should react if a language model were to discover vulnerabilities in cryptosystems where attacks do have an immediate real-world impact." Source

But wouldn't P = NP do a great deal of good?

Yes. A practical method would find the best answer to almost any question whose answers can be checked, a proof or a schedule as readily as a key. But the most powerful instrument ever made would arrive on one day, in one company's hands, and on the same day everyone else would lose the means to keep a secret or to trust a message. The good would take years to build. The harm would be ready that afternoon.

It "will make the whole internet look like a footnote in history." Fortnow

Isn't publishing how the field works?

It is, and it was hard won. In the late 1970s cryptographers were warned that publishing might bring prosecution, and a law to restrict their research was drafted, before a voluntary review was agreed. Nothing here is asked of the field. A few companies are asked not to aim a new kind of instrument at one place.

The 1977 letter, the draft bill and the review: Corrigan-Gibbs.

If the labs stop, won't a rival get there first?

Not yet, because the hardest results of this year needed what only the labs have. The Navier–Stokes proof took ten thousand agents running for 88 hours, at a cost put at six to ten million dollars for the proof alone and fifteen to twenty for the whole effort. Any state has that money. What no state has is the model it ran on, which the public cannot use. The Exponential Time Hypothesis fell to internal research models at two labs. The Riemann hypothesis fell inside a lab, to one of its own mathematicians working with its model.

So the labs would not be answering a race. They would be starting one.

And getting there first wins nothing. The method reads other people's secrets and cannot guard your own. Whoever holds it is exactly as exposed as everyone else on the day a second party has it, and it is a few pages of mathematics.

That is why the commitment is conditional. If anyone is found running such a campaign, everyone is released, and the labs that held back are the ones with the systems to finish first. Whoever starts that race brings the strongest runners into it, and loses their own locks at the end of it.

Why not prove it, and simply refrain from making it fast?

Because a proof cannot be kept. It would leak, or be found again, and it cannot be un-known.

From that day everyone with the means would have every reason to build the fast method first, and every reason to fear that someone else already had. No promise to leave it alone survives that. The only point at which stopping holds is before the proof.

Mathematics kept back has been found again

In the two best-documented cases, a piece of mathematics that mattered to cryptography was held inside a closed group. Both times the secret held, and both times outsiders found the same thing by themselves.

Ellis's own history on GCHQ; Merkle's account of his 1974 proposal; Coppersmith, 1994 on IBM.

Restraint has held before. A few people could do the work, they were the ones who asked for it, and the ask was specific: fission papers in 1940, recombinant DNA in 1974, mirror bacteria since 2024. The 1974 pause was tied to a stated condition and a meeting to review it.

In 1940 the physicists' own committee held back fission papers. Its organiser promised to publish them later "with a statement of the original date of the paper". Weart, 1976; Smyth Report

Mirror life, 2024. The 2023 pause letter, by its organisers' own account, paused nothing.
